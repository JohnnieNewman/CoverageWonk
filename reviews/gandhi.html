Expert Development Analysis

1.The Page 10 Exclusion Zone

The script commits a classic biographical development error by completely front-loading its text with an assassination sequence and an expansive state funeral in 1948, before executing a massive time jump back to 1893 to show Mahatma Gandhi as a young lawyer traveling on a train in South Africa. By page 12, the text is entirely consumed by localized administrative discrimination. We watch the protagonist get physically ejected from a first-class train compartment by rail guards because of his race, sit on a cold platform in silence, and look through his legal identification papers:

RAILWAY GUARDI've told you, coolies don't ride in first class. Now get your bags and move to the third-class compartment or I'll have you thrown off at the next station. GANDHISir, I am an attorney of the inner temple. I hold a valid first-class ticket, and I refuse to be treated in this manner.

For a massive historical epic, spending the entire first act on localized South African legal petitions and small-scale street burning of registration passes — before even touching the primary Indian independence movement — creates an immediate momentum deficit. The script demands an exhausting temporal investment from the reader before initiating the actual macro-level narrative.

2.Narrative Stagnation via Biographical Checklists

The entire second half of the script is severely paralyzed by its staggering chronological scale. The narrative operates almost exclusively as an episodic historical checklist: Gandhi moves to India, travels across the countryside in a train to look at poverty, launches a salt march, gets arrested by colonial authorities, sits in a prison cell spinning cotton, gets released, and repeats the process.

The text completely rejects basic principles of dialogue economy. Characters do not communicate to achieve immediate physical milestones; they speak in highly articulate, perfectly manicured, six-sentence prose essays designed to out-debate British viceroys or explain the macro-economic philosophy of non-cooperation. Relying on characters to explicitly dictate the moral meaning of the revolution across consecutive sequences completely stalls the film's kinetic pulse.

3.Prohibitive Production Scale vs. Anti-Climactic Hunger Strikes

From a physical production and financial standpoint, this sprawling draft represents an unviably high-risk operational investment. The script demands an astronomical budgetary layout to support a narrative that spans fifty years and requires massive, global set-pieces (such as the Amritsar massacre and funeral processions demanding hundreds of thousands of extras). Yet, the primary narrative engine builds toward zero physical escalation or commercial climax.

Instead, the tactical resolution of the sectarian violence relies on the protagonist lying on a cot in an apartment and refusing to eat until the secondary characters promise to stop fighting. Ending an expensive, wide-scope historical epic on a note where the primary conflict resolves via dietary refusal completely rejects standard commercial rules of dramatic action, ensuring an exceptionally restricted theatrical demographic footprint.

Mandatory Redlines

  1. Compress the Chronological Scope. Drastically restructure the script. Eliminate the entire South African prologue. Open the film directly in India following World War I to capture the reader's attention within a unified, high-stakes timeline.
  2. Consolidate the Arrest Cycles. Cut the repetitive prison and protest loops in Act II by 60%. Move his institutional internment beats into a streamlined visual montage to protect the script's narrative velocity.
  3. Enforce Dialogue Compression. Apply a strict minimalist filter to the verbal tracks. Strip out the extensive political manifestos and philosophical lectures, forcing the characters to communicate through brief, tactical verbal cuts.
