Logline

Two British track athletes — one a driven English Jew fighting systemic antisemitism, the other a devout Scottish Christian running for the glory of God — compete against immense societal expectations and personal convictions to achieve victory at the 1924 Paris Olympics.

Expert Development Analysis

1.The Page 10 Exclusion Zone

The script commits a fundamental development error by completely front-loading its text with an unannounced frame device and academic bureaucracy rather than initiating an active narrative pulse. The opening pages are heavily consumed by an older man's funeral in 1978, before resetting to 1919 to show Harold Abrahams arriving at Cambridge University. By page 12, the narrative remains stuck inside wood-paneled academic halls, showing the protagonist interacting with stuffy university masters and singing in local theatrical productions:

MASTER OF TRINITYHe's a running man, then. And an outsider, wouldn't you say? MASTER OF CAIUSAn undergraduate of great ambition, Master. Though his methods are... somewhat distinct from our traditional framework.

For a commercial sports property, spending the entire first act on institutional dining protocols, Gilbert and Sullivan rehearsals, and class-conscious academic snobbery before establishing the primary athletic rivalry creates an immediate momentum liability. The script expects the reader to endure an extensive history of British collegiate infrastructure before giving the audience a clear narrative trajectory.

2.Episodic Redundancy and Dialogue Over-Articulation

The entire second act suffers from a critical pacing paralysis due to its highly formulaic and divided structural layout. Because the text splits its focus across two independent protagonists who rarely interact, the narrative velocity is consistently fractured. The plot tracks along a repetitive, venue-by-venue mechanical loop: Eric Liddell runs a local race in the Scottish highlands and gives a religious sermon in the rain, Harold Abrahams hires an unconventional professional trainer and bickers with Cambridge dons, and the cycle resets.

The characters speak with zero subtext; they consistently deliver perfectly manicured, text-heavy paragraph blocks explicitly explaining their internal spiritual drives and nationalistic duties, which completely drains the racing sequences of authentic visceral tension.

3.Administrative Logic Failures and Anti-Climactic Sabbath Crisis

The script's central third-act conflict relies on an absolute structural vulnerability that severely compromises the momentum of the sports narrative. Having your primary religious protagonist drop out of his scheduled 100-meter Olympic event at the last minute because of a calendar conflict entirely halts the narrative engine. The subsequent resolution — where a wealthy teammate casually yields his own spot in a completely different race later in the week to accommodate Liddell — feels like an artificial, highly convenient historical anomaly rather than an earned tactical triumph.

Ending a high-budget athletic epic on a note of polite British compromise and slow-motion running sequences accompanied by electronic synthesizer tracks ensures a highly niche, dated commercial footprint that fails to scale globally.

Mandatory Redlines

  1. Unify the Protagonist Drive. Choose a singular main character — ideally Harold Abrahams — and build a traditional three-act rival architecture around him. Reduce the secondary Scottish track to a standard B-story to preserve continuous pacing.
  2. Slash Academic Padding by 50%. Audit the Cambridge sequences in Act I. Strip out the extensive theatrical rehearsals and administrative lifestyle details to put the characters on the track by page 10.
  3. Re-Engineer the Olympic Climax. Eliminate the scheduling-conflict dropout mechanic. Force the two leads into a direct, high-stakes, head-to-head physical race in the final act to ensure a marketable, emotionally satisfying sports movie payoff.
